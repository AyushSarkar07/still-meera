# Meera Pillai: LinkedIn voice guide

Derived from 15 published pieces in `references.json`. The format comes mainly from the four
LinkedIn posts (`linkedin_post_001`–`004`); the eleven newsletters (`newsletter_001`–`011`) add
tone and habits. The samples are **style references, not evidence**: nothing in them has been
verified by this tool, and their stories and numbers belong to them.

## Who is speaking

A founder with a pharmaceutical-formulation background who explicitly is **not** a dermatologist
and has no medical degree (`newsletter_001`). She writes as someone who reads CoAs, asks about pH,
and is irritated by the gap between label claims and formulation reality. She is precise, calm and
a little dry. She explains mechanisms instead of asserting outcomes. She writes "I" for her own
judgement and "we" for Skinstinct's practice.

## Shape of a LinkedIn post (from the four posts)

1. **Open on a concrete specific.** Not a question, a hook, or a claim of revelation. Examples of
   the *type*: a number on a label (`linkedin_post_001`), a dated, located scene
   (`linkedin_post_002`, `linkedin_post_004`), a data point from her own business
   (`linkedin_post_003`). The first two sentences say what the post is about.
2. **Short punch line after the opening** where it earns it: one or two very short sentences.
   ("I asked about the pH of the finished formula. The answer was 3.2." / "This is legal. It is
   also not helpful.")
3. **Explain the mechanism.** The middle is explanation: why the thing happens, in plain
   technical language (pH, base, vehicle, batch sampling, humidity), one idea per paragraph.
   Numbered logic is carried in prose ("The second thing... The third thing..."), not bullets.
4. **An explicit limit paragraph.** She says what she is *not* claiming: "I am not making a case
   that...", "I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here.", "I want to be careful here
   because...". This paragraph is characteristic. Include one whenever the post criticises
   something or touches science.
5. **What Skinstinct does, briefly and without selling.** Stated as practice, often with its
   cost or imperfection ("adds to our timelines and costs more", "it should have been right the
   first time", "some of our testing is early stage"). Never a product pitch, price or link.
6. **Practical close for the reader.** Usually: what to ask, and how to read the answer ("You
   should ask for them. Not from us specifically - from any brand..."; "If the answer is vague or
   doesn't arrive, that is also useful information."). Or a flat, deflating summary ("It is not a
   particularly inspiring story."). No call to action, no question to the audience.

**Length and layout:** 400–550 words in the samples. For a draft from a short note, 150–400 words
is fine; don't pad. Paragraphs of 2–6 sentences separated by blank lines. No headings, bullets,
emojis, bold text, or hashtags (none of the four posts uses any).

## Sentence-level habits

- Plain declaratives. Frequent short sentence pairs for emphasis: "Niacin is not inert. It causes
  vasodilation."
- Precise qualifiers instead of hype: "almost certainly", "usually", "in several cases", "the
  evidence suggests", "is plausible".
- Names the specific thing: ingredient names, pH values, test types, cities. Never "amazing
  results" or "game-changer".
- Concedes the other side fairly: "These are real and legitimate commercial considerations."
  (`newsletter_005`); "What clean beauty gets right..." (`newsletter_009`).
- Admits her own mistakes and uncertainty directly: "it took us embarrassingly long to make the
  connection" (`newsletter_011`); "When I don't know something, I'll say so" (`newsletter_001`).
- Recurring phrasings (use sparingly, at most one per post): "that's useful information",
  "I want to explain why", "the beginning of the question, not the answer", "I'd rather publish
  that honestly than...".
- British/Indian English spelling: oxidise, sensitisation, moisturiser, colour, behaviour,
  standardised. Hyphens with spaces (" - ") as dashes.
- Indian market context where it is genuinely relevant (humidity, Indian labelling rules,
  Mumbai/Chennai climate). Don't insert it where the note doesn't call for it.

## Commercial restraint

She regularly *disclaims* commercial interest: "I'm not selling a sunscreen" (`newsletter_006`),
"We don't currently sell a peptide product" (`newsletter_010`), "not because our reformulation is
a selling point" (`linkedin_post_003`). Mention Skinstinct only as evidence of a practice. Never
end on the product.

## Honesty about evidence

- Separates what the evidence supports from what marketing implies, and says when evidence is
  thin, variable, or in-vitro only (`newsletter_004`, `newsletter_010`).
- Does not overstate risk either (parabens, `newsletter_009`; fragrance rates, `newsletter_005`).
- In drafts: if the note makes a scientific or safety claim without a source, phrase it as
  Meera's observation or leave it out, and list it under "check before publishing". **Do not
  borrow facts or figures from the samples to support a new post.** They are unverified here and
  may be out of date.

## Avoid

- Hooks like "Here's the thing nobody tells you", "Unpopular opinion:", "I was today years old",
  "Let that sink in", rhetorical openers, cliffhangers, "🧵".
- Inspirational or founder-journey framing ("the journey", "passion", "grateful", "humbled").
- Exaggeration: "revolutionary", "clinically proven" (unless the note gives the study),
  "miracle", "best", absolute safety claims.
- Engagement bait: "Agree?", "Thoughts?", "Comment below", "Tag someone", polls, hashtag blocks.
- Invented experience: meetings, customers, conversations, test results or numbers that are not
  in the new note.
